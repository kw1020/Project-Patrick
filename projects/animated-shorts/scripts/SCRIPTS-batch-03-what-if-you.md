## 06. What if you were the last person alive?

**Facts:** Without people, power plants shut down within hours to days (no fuel deliveries or operators). New York's
subway needs pumps running constantly; Alan Weisman, "The World Without Us" (2007), citing MTA engineers: tunnels flood
in about 36 hours to two days. Without light pollution, the Milky Way is visible from cities (the 1994 Los Angeles
blackout famously had residents calling about the "strange cloud"). Fresh food spoils in days to weeks; canned food lasts
years. Untreated gasoline goes stale in roughly 3-12 months. Within decades, plants crack roads and buildings (Weisman).

**Look:** an empty city street, a shopping mall, the subway flooding, the Milky Way over dark skyscrapers, a bookshop.

> What if you woke up one day, and you were the last person alive? For the first week, it's actually kind of great.
> Every shop is open. Every car is yours. There's no line for anything.
> But nobody's running the power plants, so one by one, they shut down.
> By the second night, the whole city is dark, and for the first time in your life, you can see the Milky Way from downtown.
> In New York, the subway tunnels flood in about two days, because nobody's running the pumps.
> All the fresh food rots in a few weeks, so it's canned beans from now on.
> After a year or so, the gas in every car goes stale, and your new collection of sports cars won't even start.
> Luckily, you own every bookshop on Earth.
> Unluckily, you'll be reading them all by candlelight.
> You see, a city isn't really a thing. It's a job. Millions of people fixing millions of tiny problems, every single day.
> It's like a sandcastle at the beach. Stop patting it back into shape, and the waves take it, a little more every day.
> Within a few decades, trees are pushing up through the middle of the highway.
> But on the bright side, you'd finally have time to read the terms and conditions.
> All of them.

## 07. What if you could run at the speed of sound?

**Facts:** Speed of sound ≈ 343 m/s ≈ 1,235 km/h. Air hitting you at that speed heats by about v²/2c_p ≈ 58 °C, so on a
20 °C day the air at your face reaches about 80 °C. Drag power = ½ρC_dAv³ ≈ 0.5 × 1.2 × 1.0 × 0.7 × 343³ ≈ 17 MW ≈ 4,000
kcal every second, about two days of food (2,000 kcal/day). Drag power grows with speed cubed (twice as fast = 8×). A
12 mg fly at 343 m/s carries about 0.7 J, like a BB pellet. Human reaction ≈ 0.25 s → about 85 m travelled before you
react. Stopping from 343 m/s in 50 m ≈ 120 g; trained fighter pilots in g-suits black out around 9 g. Sonic booms can
break windows.

**Look:** a city street, a blur of a runner (our own character), shattering windows, a lamppost far behind, a fridge.

> What if you could run at the speed of sound? Getting started would be easy. Everything after that would be a problem.
> You lean forward and go. In a second, you're doing twelve hundred kilometres an hour.
> The air in front of you gets squashed so hard it heats up to about eighty degrees. It's like running face-first into a hair dryer.
> Every bug you hit stings like a BB pellet.
> Behind you, a sonic boom rattles every window on the street, and a few of them shatter.
> And by the time your brain even notices a lamppost, you've already gone eighty metres past it.
> Luckily, you can cross the whole city in under a minute.
> Unluckily, every second of running burns about two days' worth of food.
> You see, the faster you go, the harder the air pushes back. Go twice as fast, and you need eight times the power.
> It's like sticking your hand out of a car window. In a parking lot, nothing. On the highway, it nearly takes your arm off.
> And stopping? Braking that hard in fifty metres would hit you with over a hundred g's. Fighter pilots black out at about nine.
> So the scariest part of super speed isn't the running.
> It's the grocery bill.

## 08. What if your reactions were ten times faster?

**Facts:** Typical human visual reaction time ≈ 0.25 s. A 100 mph (161 km/h) fastball reaches the plate in about 0.4 s
(×10 = 4 s felt). World Athletics treats a start reaction under 0.100 s as a false start. Human flicker fusion is
roughly 50-90 Hz (simplified to "about sixty pictures a second"); films run at 24 frames per second; many lights flicker
at 100 or 120 times a second. Houseflies resolve flicker up to about 250 Hz (about four times us), which is why they
dodge a swatter. A 2-minute microwave timer felt 10× slower = 20 minutes.

**Look:** a kitchen in slow motion, a fly hanging in the air, a baseball field, a running track, a living room movie
night, a strobing ceiling light.

> What if your reactions were ten times faster? The whole world would turn into slow motion.
> Right now, it takes you about a quarter of a second to react to anything.
> At ten times faster, a fly buzzing past looks like it's floating. You just pluck it out of the air.
> A hundred-mile-an-hour fastball takes a lazy four seconds to reach you. You could check your phone first.
> At the Olympics, you'd be disqualified from every race, because the rules say nobody can react that fast, so you must have cheated.
> Then you go home to watch a movie, and it's a slideshow.
> And every light in the house flickers like a strobe.
> Luckily, you'd never drop your phone again.
> Unluckily, waiting two minutes for the microwave would feel like twenty.
> You see, your brain takes snapshots of the world, roughly sixty every second. Anything faster than that blurs together.
> Movies and lights change faster than your brain can keep up, so they look smooth.
> It's like a flipbook. Flip it fast, and the drawing moves. Flip it slowly, and it's just a stack of pictures.
> A housefly already sees the world about four times faster than you. That's why you can never swat one.
> To a fly, your hand is moving in slow motion.

## 09. What if the world just ended?

**Facts:** 66 million years ago, an asteroid about 10 km wide hit near Chicxulub, Mexico, at about 20 km/s (~70,000
km/h). Crater ≈ 180 km wide. Energy ≈ 100 million megatons, billions of times the Hiroshima bomb. Ejecta rained back
down worldwide; widespread fires; tsunamis far taller than skyscrapers; soot and sulfur darkened the sky for years,
collapsing food chains. About 75 % of species went extinct, including all non-bird dinosaurs. NASA reports finding more
than 90 % of near-Earth asteroids larger than 1 km, none on a collision course for at least the next century. NASA's
DART spacecraft hit the asteroid Dimorphos on 26 Sept 2022 and shortened its orbit by about 32 minutes.

**Look:** a sunny prehistoric valley with our own cartoon dinosaurs, a streak in the sky, the impact, a dark sky,
a modern observatory, a spacecraft hitting a rock.

> What if the world just ended? Say a rock ten kilometres wide slams into the Earth. We know exactly what happens, because it already did.
> Sixty-six million years ago, a space rock the size of a city hits Mexico at seventy thousand kilometres an hour.
> It blasts out a crater a hundred and eighty kilometres wide.
> The explosion is like billions of atomic bombs going off at once.
> Melted rock rains down all over the planet, and forests catch fire.
> A tsunami taller than a skyscraper races across the ocean.
> Then the sky goes dark with soot and dust, for years.
> Three out of every four kinds of animals and plants disappear, including the dinosaurs.
> Luckily, today we watch the sky. We've found almost every giant asteroid out there, and none of them are coming for us.
> Unluckily for the dinosaurs, they didn't have a space program.
> You see, the rock itself didn't get most of them. The darkness did. No sunlight, no plants. No plants, no plant-eaters. No plant-eaters, no T. rex.
> It's like pulling the bottom block out of a Jenga tower.
> And in 2022, NASA tested a plan. It crashed a spacecraft into an asteroid on purpose, and knocked it off course.
> So if a big one ever does come for us, the plan is simple. We hit it first.
