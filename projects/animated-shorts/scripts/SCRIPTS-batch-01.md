# Batch 01: five "crazy true history" scripts (v1, 2026-11-16)

Format: claude-animated-shorts formula (hook, setting, escalation, snap, "Luckily / Unluckily",
one everyday analogy, consequence, callback twist). ~3.7 words/s, aim 55-70 s.
Rules from the skill: no captions in the video, one steady voice, jokes are real facts,
never describe what the viewer already sees. Story, characters and voice are our own.
Each script lists its facts and sources first. Check any fact you're unsure of before recording.

## 01. The Emu War (1932)

**Facts:** Nov-Dec 1932, Campion district, Western Australia. ~20,000 emus moved onto wheat farms.
Defence Minister Sir George Pearce sent Major G.P.W. Meredith (Royal Australian Artillery), two soldiers,
two Lewis machine guns and 10,000 rounds. 2 Nov: emus out of range. 4 Nov: ~1,000 emus near a dam, gun
jammed after about a dozen. A gun mounted on a truck couldn't keep up or aim on rough ground; emus run
up to ~50 km/h. Meredith claimed 986 kills for 9,860 rounds (~10 per bird) and said an army with "the
bullet-carrying capacity of these birds" would "face any army in the world". Farmers asked for the army
again in 1934, 1943 and 1948; refused. The emu is on Australia's coat of arms.
Sources: Wikipedia "Emu War"; Australian War Memorial.

**Look:** dusty golden wheat fields, a tiny army truck, a sea of emus.

> In 1932, the Australian army declared war on birds. And lost.
> Twenty thousand emus had just moved onto the wheat farms of Western Australia, and they were eating everything.
> So the government sent in a major, two soldiers, two machine guns and ten thousand bullets.
> On the first day, the emus saw them coming and calmly walked out of range.
> Two days later, a thousand emus walked right up to a watering hole. The gun jammed.
> So the soldiers bolted a machine gun onto the back of a truck and gave chase.
> The emus ran at fifty kilometres an hour, straight across the bumpiest ground they could find.
> The truck bounced so hard that nobody could aim.
> Luckily, the army had brought ten thousand bullets.
> Unluckily, it took about ten bullets per emu.
> You see, a flock of emus isn't one big target. The moment there's danger, it splits into tiny groups running in every direction.
> Ever tried to pick up a bag of marbles you just spilled? Grab one, and the other ninety-nine are already under the sofa.
> The major said if he had an army that could take bullets like these birds, he could face any army in the world.
> The farmers asked for the army three more times. The answer was always no.
> And today, the emu is on Australia's coat of arms. The machine gun isn't.

## 02. The 1904 Olympic Marathon

**Facts:** 30 Aug 1904, St. Louis. ~90°F, dirt roads, cars ahead raising dust. 32 started, 14 finished.
Organiser James Sullivan wanted to study dehydration, so water was only at a water tower (mile 6) and a
well (mile 12). Len Taunyane was chased about a mile off course by wild dogs. Andarín Carvajal ate rotten
apples from an orchard, napped, and finished 4th. Thomas Hicks's trainers gave him strychnine (rat poison)
with egg whites and brandy; he won in 3:28:53 and was held up by his trainers at the finish. Fred Lorz
rode about 11 miles in a car, crossed first, was exposed, banned for life; the ban was lifted and he won
the Boston Marathon in April 1905.
Sources: Wikipedia "Athletics at the 1904 Summer Olympics – Men's marathon"; Smithsonian Magazine.

**Look:** hot hazy dirt road, dust clouds behind old cars, an orchard, a stadium.

> The 1904 Olympic marathon was won by a man whose team kept feeding him rat poison.
> It's St. Louis, it's ninety degrees, and the course is twenty-four miles of dirt road.
> Cars drive ahead of the runners, so everybody's breathing dust the whole way.
> And the organiser wants to study what thirst does to people, so there's water at exactly two places on the course.
> One runner gets chased a mile off the course by wild dogs.
> Another stops at an orchard, eats some rotten apples, and takes a nap. He still finishes fourth.
> Thomas Hicks is fading, so his trainers give him strychnine, which is rat poison, washed down with egg whites and brandy.
> But the first man into the stadium is Fred Lorz, fresh as a daisy.
> Luckily for Fred, he crossed the line first.
> Unluckily for Fred, people had seen him riding in a car for eleven of those miles.
> You see, your body cools itself by sweating, and every drop of sweat is water you have to put back.
> It's like driving on a hot day with no water in the radiator. It runs fine, right up until it really doesn't.
> Hicks got the gold. His trainers had to hold him up across the line.
> Today, a marathon has water every few kilometres.
> And Fred Lorz was banned for life. The ban lasted about a year, and the next spring he won the Boston Marathon. On foot.

## 03. The Leaning Tower of Pisa

**Facts:** Building began 1173; it started sinking on its soft south side by 1178, a few floors up.
Wars stopped work for almost a century, which let the soil firm up. Later builders made the upper floors
taller on the short side, so the tower is curved. Finished 1372. In 1934 Mussolini ordered it
straightened; concrete grout pumped into the foundation made it lean more. By 1990 the top overhung by
about 4.5 m and it closed. Engineers removed ~38 m³ of soil from under the north side; when it reopened
in 2001 it was ~45 cm straighter. They aimed to stabilise it, not straighten it.
Sources: Wikipedia "Leaning Tower of Pisa"; Burland et al., Pisa stabilisation reports.

**Look:** white marble tower, Italian piazza, scaffolding, a 1930s concrete crew.

> The most famous building in Italy is famous because its builders got the ground wrong.
> It's 1173, and Pisa is rich enough to build a marble bell tower next to its cathedral.
> Five years in, with just a few floors built, the whole thing starts sinking on one side.
> Then Pisa goes to war, and nobody touches the tower for almost a hundred years.
> When the builders come back, they have a plan: make every new floor a little taller on the short side.
> In the 1930s, Mussolini orders it fixed, and workers pump concrete into the foundation.
> It leans even further.
> By 1990, the top is hanging more than four metres out, and they close it to visitors.
> Luckily, the builders had found a way to correct the lean.
> Unluckily, it means the tower doesn't just lean. It's bent like a banana.
> You see, the tower sits on soft clay and sand, and the south side is softer than the north.
> It's like standing on a mattress on one foot. The more weight you put down, the more it squishes, and the more you tip.
> So in the end, engineers didn't push the tower. They gently dug soil out from under the high side and let it settle back.
> When it reopened in 2001, it stood forty-five centimetres straighter.
> They could have kept going. They stopped on purpose, because a straight Tower of Pisa is just a tower.

## 04. The Vasa (1628)

**Facts:** Ordered by King Gustavus Adolphus: 64 guns on two gun decks, ~700 painted carvings. Before
launch, Admiral Klas Fleming had 30 men run across the deck; he stopped after three runs because it heeled
so much. 10 Aug 1628: crowds watched it set off in Stockholm harbour, firing a salute; a gust of wind heeled
it, water poured in through the open lower gun ports, and it sank after about 1,300 m. About 30 people
died. Baltic water is too low in salt for shipworms, so it survived. Raised in 1961, 333 years later,
almost entirely original. The Vasa Museum is the most visited museum in Scandinavia.
Sources: Vasa Museum (vasamuseet.se); Wikipedia "Vasa (ship)".

**Look:** huge carved wooden warship, Stockholm harbour, crowds on the shore, cold green water.

> In 1628, Sweden built the most powerful warship in the world. It sailed just over a kilometre.
> The king wants a monster: two whole decks of cannons, sixty-four guns, and hundreds of painted carvings.
> Before launch, the admiral runs a test: thirty sailors sprint from one side of the deck to the other.
> On the first run, the ship rocks. On the second, it rocks harder. On the third, they stop the test.
> Nobody wants to tell the king.
> So on launch day, crowds line the harbour. The cannons fire a salute, and the sails go up.
> Then a gust of wind tips it over, water pours in through the lowest gun ports, and the Vasa sinks right in front of everyone.
> About thirty people didn't make it.
> Luckily, it sank in the cold Baltic, where the water isn't salty enough for ship-eating worms.
> Unluckily, that means everyone can still see exactly how badly it was built.
> You see, a ship stays upright when its heavy stuff is low and its body is wide. The Vasa had heavy cannons up high and a narrow hull.
> Try standing on a skateboard while holding a bowling ball over your head. One little nudge and you're on the floor.
> Three hundred and thirty-three years later, Sweden pulled it back up, almost perfectly preserved.
> Today it's the most visited museum in Scandinavia.
> The ship that couldn't stay up for one afternoon has now been on display for over sixty years.

## 05. The Great Molasses Flood (1919)

**Facts:** 15 Jan 1919, North End, Boston. Purity Distilling tank: 50 ft tall, 90 ft wide, ~2.3 million
US gallons. It leaked badly; residents collected the leaks; the company painted it brown. It was never
tested with water. The temperature had risen from about 2°F to above 40°F. Around 12:30 pm rivets
popped "like a machine gun", the tank burst, and a wave up to 25 ft high moved at ~35 mph, damaging the
elevated railway and buildings. 21 died, ~150 injured. As it cooled it thickened, hampering rescue.
Fireboats washed streets with seawater; the harbour stayed brown into summer. The court found the company
liable; building rules tightened to require engineers' signed calculations. Residents said the area
smelled of molasses on hot days for decades.
Sources: Wikipedia "Great Molasses Flood"; Smithsonian Magazine; Stephen Puleo, "Dark Tide".

**Tone:** people died, so the jokes stay on the irony (the saying, the paint, the smell), never on victims.

**Look:** 1919 Boston brick streets, elevated railway, a giant tank, a glossy brown wave.

> There's an old saying: slow as molasses in January. In January 1919, molasses hit Boston at thirty-five miles an hour.
> In the middle of the North End stands a steel tank five storeys tall, holding over two million gallons of molasses.
> It leaks so badly that locals collect the drips, and the company's answer is to paint the tank brown so nobody notices.
> Then one January morning, the weather jumps from freezing to almost spring.
> At lunchtime, the rivets start popping out like gunshots.
> Then the whole tank splits open, and a brown wave taller than a house rolls down the street.
> It bends the steel of the elevated railway and knocks buildings off their foundations.
> Twenty-one people were killed.
> Luckily, molasses slows down as it cools.
> Unluckily, that's exactly what made the rescue so hard.
> You see, molasses gets thicker the colder it gets. By evening, the flood had set like glue, and rescuers had to wade through it.
> It's like honey straight from the fridge. Warm, it pours. Cold, it holds your spoon in place.
> In court, it came out that the tank had never even been tested, and the rules changed: big structures now need an engineer to sign off on the maths.
> Fireboats hosed the streets with sea water, and the harbour stayed brown until summer.
> And for decades, people in the North End said that on a hot day, you could still smell the molasses.
