# Batch 02: five "What if" science scripts (v1, 2026-11-16)

Niche (Krew, 2026-11-16): **"What if" science.** History is parked after the Emu War.
Same formula as batch 01: hook (the question + a surprising answer), setup, escalation, snap, "Luckily / Unluckily",
one everyday comparison, consequence, twist ending. ~3.7 words/s, 55-70 s. No captions in the video, one steady
voice, every claim is real science (sources listed first). Where science gives a range, the script uses the
safe end ("about", "over").

## 01. What if you stopped sleeping?

**Facts:** Randy Gardner, 17, San Diego, stayed awake 264.4 hours (about 11 days), Dec 1963 to Jan 1964, for a
high-school science fair, without stimulants; Stanford sleep researcher William Dement joined to watch him. By day 2
he had trouble focusing his eyes; by day 4 he mistook a street sign for a person and believed he was pro football
player Paul Lowe. He slept about 14 hours afterwards. Being awake 24 h impairs performance about as much as a
0.10 % blood alcohol level (Dawson & Reid, Nature 1997). During sleep, fluid flushes waste from the brain faster
(Xie et al., Science 2013, the "glymphatic" system). Rats kept totally awake died in about 2-4 weeks (Rechtschaffen
et al. 1989). Guinness stopped accepting sleep-deprivation records as too dangerous. Gardner later said he had
insomnia in later life (NPR, 2017).

**Look:** a 1960s teen bedroom, a kitchen at night, a street with a sign that turns into a person, a brain "kitchen".

> What if you just stopped sleeping? In 1963, a teenager actually tried it.
> Seventeen-year-old Randy Gardner decides to stay awake for his school science fair. No coffee, no pills, just friends keeping him up.
> After one day without sleep, your brain works about as well as someone who's legally drunk.
> By day two, Randy can't focus his eyes.
> By day four, he's arguing with a street sign, because he thinks it's a person.
> Then he decides he's a famous professional football player.
> A sleep scientist from Stanford starts following him around, just to make sure he's okay.
> Luckily, Randy made it to two hundred and sixty-four hours and set a world record.
> Unluckily, it was so dangerous that Guinness stopped accepting the record altogether.
> You see, while you sleep, your brain runs a cleaning crew. Fluid washes out the waste that piles up while you're awake.
> It's like a restaurant kitchen. Cook all day and never clean up, and pretty soon you can't find a single clean pan.
> In the lab, rats that were kept awake didn't make it past a few weeks.
> Randy went to bed and slept for fourteen hours.
> And years later, the boy who stayed awake for eleven days said his biggest problem was insomnia.

## 02. What if the Moon disappeared?

**Facts:** The Moon causes most of Earth's tides; the Sun's tidal pull is about 46 % of the Moon's, so without the
Moon tides would shrink to less than half. Many corals on the Great Barrier Reef spawn together a few nights after a
full moon in late spring. Some dung beetles navigate by moonlight (Dacke et al., Nature 2003). The Moon's tides slow
Earth's spin, which is why days have lengthened over hundreds of millions of years. The Moon helps keep Earth's tilt
steady; without it the tilt could vary much more over millions of years (Laskar et al. 1993; later work suggests
less extreme swings, so the script says "could"). The Moon is drifting away by about 3.8 cm per year (lunar laser
ranging), about as fast as fingernails grow.

**Look:** a seaside town at night, a beach, a coral reef at night, a dung beetle, Earth from space.

> What if the Moon disappeared tonight? You wouldn't feel a thing. The ocean would.
> It's a normal night in a seaside town. The Moon is up, the tide is coming in.
> Then the Moon is just gone.
> The night gets darker than anyone in town has ever seen.
> The next morning, the tide barely comes in at all.
> On the reef, millions of corals that only spawn after a full moon just sit there waiting.
> A dung beetle that steers home by moonlight walks in circles.
> And fishermen who've read the tides their whole lives can't read them anymore.
> Luckily, the Sun makes tides too.
> Unluckily, they're less than half the size.
> You see, the Moon's gravity pulls the ocean toward it, and the whole planet spins underneath that bulge of water.
> It's like dragging your hand through a bathtub. Take your hand out, and the waves die down.
> Over millions of years, scientists think, Earth's tilt could start to wobble, and the seasons with it.
> But don't panic. The Moon can't just vanish.
> It is leaving, though. About four centimetres a year, roughly as fast as your fingernails grow.

## 03. What if you jumped into a hole through the Earth?

**Facts:** Earth's diameter is about 12,742 km. The deepest hole ever drilled, the Kola Superdeep Borehole, reached
12,262 m by 1989 after about 20 years of drilling: about a tenth of one percent of the way through. The centre of the
Earth is around 5,000-6,000 °C, about as hot as the surface of the Sun, at about 3.6 million atmospheres. Falling
through an airless tunnel takes about 38-42 minutes (42 min for a uniform Earth; Klotz, Am. J. Phys. 2015, gives
38 min for the real density), top speed at the centre over 28,000 km/h, and you stop exactly at the far end, then
fall back: an oscillation. With air you'd hit terminal velocity and eventually settle at the centre. The opposite
point to most of the USA is in the Indian Ocean.

**Look:** a backyard with a shovel, a 3D cutaway Earth (crust, mantle, glowing core), a falling cartoon guy, ocean.

> What if you dug a hole straight through the Earth and jumped in? You'd pop out the other side in about forty minutes.
> First, the digging. The deepest hole humans have ever drilled took about twenty years and went twelve kilometres down.
> That's about a tenth of one percent of the way.
> Keep going, and the rock gets hotter and hotter.
> At the centre, it's about as hot as the surface of the Sun, and the pressure would crush a submarine like a soda can.
> But say you've got a magic, fireproof, airless tunnel. You jump.
> For twenty minutes, you just keep speeding up. By the centre, you're doing over twenty-eight thousand kilometres an hour.
> Then you start slowing down, all the way up, until you stop, right at the lip of the hole on the other side.
> Luckily, you made it through the whole planet.
> Unluckily, from most of America, the other end is in the middle of the Indian Ocean.
> You see, gravity always pulls you toward the centre. It speeds you up on the way down and slows you down on the way up, by exactly the same amount.
> It's like a marble rolling around a bowl. It never quite gets out.
> Miss the edge, and you fall all the way back home. Then back again. Forever.
> Unless there's air in the tunnel. Then every trip is a little shorter, until days later you're just floating in the middle of the Earth.

## 04. What if the Sun stopped shining?

**Facts:** Sunlight takes about 8 minutes 20 seconds to reach Earth. If the Sun stopped shining but kept its mass,
Earth's orbit would not change. The Moon and the planets shine by reflected sunlight, so they would go dark too.
Estimates put Earth's average surface temperature below -18 °C (0 °F) within about a week and below -73 °C
(-100 °F) within a year. Photosynthesis stops; most plants die within weeks to months. Ocean ice insulates the water
beneath, so the deep ocean stays liquid for a very long time, and life around hydrothermal vents, which runs on
chemical energy from Earth's heat, could keep going. The Sun has about 5 billion years of fuel left.

**Look:** a sunny town, the Sun going dark, a dark sky full of stars, frost spreading, a frozen ocean, a deep-sea vent.

> What if the Sun stopped shining, right now? For about eight minutes, you wouldn't even know.
> That's how long sunlight takes to get here. So the last light is already on its way.
> Eight minutes later, the sky goes black.
> The Moon goes dark too, because moonlight is just sunlight bouncing off it. So do the planets.
> Within a week, the whole planet is colder than a freezer.
> Plants can't make food, so the forests start dying.
> Within a year, it's colder than the coldest night ever measured in most of the world.
> Luckily, Earth wouldn't fly off into space. The Sun's gravity is still there.
> Unluckily, that means we'd keep circling a giant, freezing, invisible ball in the dark.
> But the oceans would surprise you. The top would freeze solid, and the water underneath would stay liquid for a very long time.
> It's like a lake in winter. The ice on top works like a blanket.
> And deep down, next to volcanic vents, there are creatures that never needed sunlight in the first place. They'd keep going like nothing happened.
> The good news? The Sun can't just switch off. It has about five billion years of fuel left.
> So you've got time to buy a jacket.

## 05. What if you shrank to the size of an ant?

**Facts:** Square-cube law: shrink by a factor k and strength (muscle cross-section) drops by k² while weight drops by
k³, so pound for pound you get k times stronger. From 1.75 m to about 2 mm, k is roughly 875. J.B.S. Haldane, "On
Being the Right Size" (1926): you can drop a mouse down a thousand-yard mine shaft and it walks away; small things
reach a low top speed in air. Haldane: a wet person carries about a pound of water, a wet fly many times its weight;
surface tension makes water a trap for tiny animals. Small bodies lose heat fast: the Etruscan shrew (about 2 g) eats
about twice its body weight a day and its heart can beat over 1,500 times a minute. A voice's pitch scales roughly with
vocal fold size; shrunk 875 times, 120 Hz becomes about 100,000 Hz, far above human hearing (about 20,000 Hz).

**Look:** a kitchen floor from ant height (re-use the porch set and the marbles as giant boulders), a crumb, a raindrop,
a table edge, a cat.

> What if you shrank to the size of an ant? You'd suddenly be one of the strongest things in the room.
> One second you're standing in your kitchen. The next, a crumb is the size of a sofa.
> You walk over and lift it. Easily.
> Then you walk off the edge of the table by accident. You float down, land, and you're fine.
> You yell for help, but your voice is so high-pitched that nobody can hear it.
> And you're starving, because tiny bodies lose heat so fast that you'd need to eat constantly.
> Then it starts to rain.
> Luckily, you can fall off a skyscraper and walk away.
> Unluckily, a single raindrop could hold you down like glue.
> You see, when you shrink, your weight drops much faster than your strength. And air slows small things down so much that falling barely hurts.
> But water clings to everything. At your size, getting wet is like climbing out of a pool wearing a coat made of water.
> It's why a tiny shrew has to eat twice its own weight every day, and why insects avoid puddles like we avoid cliffs.
> So the next time you see an ant carrying a crumb way bigger than it is, remember.
> It's not that ants are strong. You're just huge.
